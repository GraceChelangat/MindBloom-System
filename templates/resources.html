UNDERSTANDING

What is Anorexia Nervosa?

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a serious eating disorder and mental health condition characterized by persistent restriction of food intake, an intense fear of gaining weight or behaviours that interfere with weight gain, and a distorted perception of body weight or shape.

People experiencing anorexia nervosa may place excessive importance on their body weight or appearance when evaluating themselves.

AN is not simply about food or wanting to be thin. It is a complex condition involving psychological, biological, emotional and social factors.

1. Main characteristics

  1. Restriction of food intake: Consistently eating less than the body needs, potentially leading to significantly low body weight.
  2. Fear of weight gain: Experiencing intense anxiety about gaining weight, even when underweight.
  3. Distorted body image: Perceiving one's body inaccurately or placing excessive importance on weight and shape.
  4. Eating-related behaviours: Following rigid food rules, avoiding meals or engaging in other behaviours that interfere with adequate nourishment.
  5. Emotional difficulties: Experiencing anxiety, depression, guilt, perfectionism or difficulties regulating emotions.

2. Types of anorexia nervosa

Commonly described clinical subtypes
TypeDescription
Restricting type (AN-R)Weight loss occurs primarily through dietary restriction, fasting or excessive exercise, without recurrent binge-eating or purging episodes during the relevant assessment period.

3. Effects of anorexia nervosa

AN can affect several areas of health:

  • Physical: Fatigue, dizziness, hormonal changes, weakened bones and cardiovascular complications.
  • Psychological: Anxiety, depression, difficulty concentrating and persistent concerns about food or body image.
  • Social: Withdrawal from friends and family, relationship difficulties and reduced participation in everyday activities.
  • Academic: Reduced concentration, memory difficulties and declining performance.

Some complications can become life-threatening. Recovery is possible with appropriate medical, nutritional and psychological treatment.

4. Relapse and possible warning signs

Relapse refers to the return or significant worsening of eating-disorder symptoms after a period of improvement or recovery.

Possible warning signs include increased food restriction, renewed distress about body image, withdrawal from social activities and a return to harmful eating-related behaviours.

These signs do not by themselves confirm a relapse. A qualified professional can assess what is happening and help determine appropriate support.

5. Why this matters to MindBloom

MindBloom is a web-based project exploring mood, eating behaviours and potential relapse-related patterns among teenagers and young adults. It supports reflection on:

  • Mood patterns: Emotional changes such as sadness, anxiety and stress.
  • Eating behaviours: Self-reported eating difficulties and changes in eating patterns.
  • Sleep patterns: Changes in sleep duration and quality.
  • Behavioural trends: Changes in recorded experiences over time.
  • Potential warning signs: Patterns that may warrant further professional assessment.

These records may help users reflect and provide longitudinal information for conversations with professionals. Accurate relapse prediction requires an appropriately validated model and clinical evidence; the current exploratory food-craving model does not establish relapse risk.

RECOVERY

Anorexia Nervosa Recovery

Recovery means restoring physical health, developing healthier eating behaviours, improving psychological wellbeing and rebuilding a balanced relationship with food, body image and everyday life.

It is not simply about gaining weight. Recovery also addresses emotional, psychological and behavioural factors that contribute to an eating disorder.

The stages often overlap, and recovery is not always linear. Care should be individualized and guided by qualified professionals.

1. Stages of recovery

  1. Recognition and acceptance: Recognizing the eating disorder and understanding the need for professional support.
  2. Medical stabilization: Addressing malnutrition, physical complications and immediate health concerns under medical supervision.
  3. Nutritional rehabilitation: Restoring adequate, consistent nutrition and gradually establishing more flexible eating patterns.
  4. Psychological recovery: Addressing fear of weight gain, body-image distress, anxiety and unhelpful thinking patterns through therapy.
  5. Behavioural recovery: Reducing restrictive eating, compulsive exercise and other eating-disorder behaviours.
  6. Maintenance and relapse prevention: Developing coping strategies, recognizing warning signs and maintaining professional and social support.

2. Important aspects of recovery

  • Physical: Restoring nutritional health, energy, hormonal functioning and overall physical stability.
  • Emotional: Learning to recognize and manage emotions without relying on harmful eating behaviours.
  • Psychological: Developing healthier beliefs about food, weight, body image and self-worth.
  • Social: Rebuilding relationships, joining social activities and reducing isolation.
  • Behavioural: Establishing consistent eating routines and reducing restrictive or compensatory behaviours.

3. Treatment approaches

Recovery typically involves coordinated care from healthcare professionals. Approaches may include:

  • Medical monitoring: Assessing physical health and managing complications.
  • Nutritional support: Working with an eating-disorder-informed dietitian to restore adequate nutrition.
  • CBT-ED: Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Eating Disorders can address eating-disorder thoughts and behaviours.
  • Family-Based Treatment: Often used with adolescents, with family members actively supporting recovery as part of care.
  • Psychological support: Addressing anxiety, depression, perfectionism and other contributing difficulties.

For someone who has been significantly restricting food, nutritional rehabilitation may need medical supervision because of complications such as refeeding syndrome.

4. Relapse during recovery

A relapse is when eating-disorder symptoms return or significantly worsen after a period of improvement.

Possible warning signs include increased food restriction, renewed fear surrounding eating, worsening body-image distress, compulsive exercise and social withdrawal.

An isolated difficult day does not necessarily indicate relapse. Clinicians assess changes in the context of a person's overall health and treatment history. Recovery can include setbacks.

6. Signs of recovery progress

Progress may include greater flexibility around food, reduced eating-related anxiety, improved energy and concentration, increased social engagement, more stable physical health and an improved ability to manage difficult emotions.

These changes may happen at different rates. Progress is best considered holistically rather than measured by a single indicator.
